01 — Opening

The answer to “tell us about your project”

They will open with this. Have one clean paragraph ready and stop talking at the end of it — let them ask the next question rather than filling silence.

Say this — 30 seconds

Fog computing puts computation close to IoT sensors so that latency-critical applications — cardiac monitoring, video surveillance — can meet their deadlines. But fog nodes are commodity hardware in uncontrolled environments, and they fail far more often than data-centre servers. Every existing fault-tolerance mechanism in fog is reactive: it detects a failure and then recovers, which costs exactly the latency the application cannot afford. We are using a Bayesian Belief Network to predict the probability that a fog node will fail before it does, and to use that probability to move critical services off the node in advance.

If they ask you to expand — 2 minutes

We surveyed five fog fault-tolerance systems. Three are purely reactive — checkpointing, ACK retransmission, heartbeat failover. One is an optimisation-based placement scheme where reliability is only a static term in a fitness function. The closest to ours, FTAPA, does migrate proactively, but it triggers on a fixed threshold applied to a single health index, and the authors themselves say the threshold is hard to tune — too low and migration happens too late, too high and you migrate unnecessarily.

The reason a fixed threshold does not work is that node health is latent. You never observe it directly. You observe CPU, memory, latency, jitter, energy, handover rate — noisy, interdependent indicators, some of which go missing exactly when the node is in trouble. That is precisely the problem Bayesian Belief Networks were built for.

So our framework has three parts. A BBN infers the probability of failure within a look-ahead window from whatever telemetry is available. That probability feeds a placement utility and a migration rule based on expected loss rather than a threshold. And it feeds a replication scheme that gives standby capacity only to modules where risk and criticality are both high, instead of provisioning a backup for everything.

What to know

If asked “what is the one sentence problem statement” — no existing fog fault-tolerance mechanism quantifies the probability of imminent node failure under uncertainty, or uses that probability to drive placement, migration and redundancy decisions before the failure occurs. That sentence is on slide 4 and in the paper abstract. Memorise it.

02 — The problem

Why this problem is worth solving

The panel will test whether the problem is real or invented. The chain of reasoning below is what makes it real — be able to walk down it.

  1. Latency-critical IoT cannot use the cloud. An ECG stream must be classified in tens of milliseconds; a tracked object leaves the frame before a distant data centre replies.
  2. So we move computation to fog nodes — gateways, routers, base stations, one or two hops from the sensor.
  3. But fog nodes are unreliable. Commodity hardware, uncontrolled environments, shared constrained uplinks, limited or battery power, and mobile users whose handovers continually reshape their load.
  4. A failure on a fog node is worse than a failure in the cloud, because there is no redundant power, cooling or network path, and because the application that was placed there was placed there precisely because it could not tolerate delay.
  5. Failures are rarely instantaneous. A node degrades — rising utilisation, thermal stress, queue build-up, link deterioration — before it becomes unresponsive.
  6. That degradation window is an opportunity, and current fog fault-tolerance research does not exploit it.
Say this — if asked “why not just use the cloud / more backups?”

Adding backups is what the existing work does, and it has two costs. The standby capacity is consumed whether or not a failure ever happens — the FTAPA authors list that as a standing limitation of their own design. And a backup still only helps after the failure: you pay the failover latency at the worst possible moment. We are trying to avoid paying it at all.

FTAPA proved that acting early helps — we are building on that, not disputing it. The difference is what drives the decision. We compute a probability with a causal model, which gives us three things a threshold cannot: it still produces an answer when a telemetry channel drops out, it tells us which condition is driving the risk, and because it is a probability we can combine it with a cost and derive the migration threshold instead of tuning it.

What to know

The one-line gap, if they want it compressed: every surveyed system either reacts to a fault that already happened, or anticipates it with a fixed threshold on a directly observed metric. None of them represents node health as a latent variable with quantified uncertainty.

03b — Inference

What the survey, taken together, tells us

The panel cares less about five summaries than about what you concluded from reading them. These six findings are the bridge from survey to design — know them as a set.

  1. Fault tolerance in fog is overwhelmingly reactive. Three of five act only after detection — a missed heartbeat [3], an unacknowledged message [2], a threshold breach during execution [1]. Recovery latency lands at the worst moment.
  2. Acting early demonstrably works. FTAPA [5] reports reduced downtime from migrating ahead of failure. The principle is sound — you build on it, you don't dispute it.
  3. But prediction is deterministic and single-metric. [1] and [5] both reduce it to one scalar against a preset threshold, and both sets of authors name the tuning difficulty as an open limitation.
  4. Node health is treated as directly observable. Nobody models it as latent — inferred from noisy, interdependent, sometimes-absent indicators — even though a channel falls silent exactly when its node is degrading.
  5. Redundancy is provisioned uniformly, not by risk. A backup per task [1], per server [3], per microservice [5] — capacity consumed whether or not failure ever comes. [5] names this as a cost of its own design.
  6. Reliability enters optimisation only as a static term. QoS-EO [4] scores it in a fitness function but never estimates whether a given node will fail in a given interval.
Say this — “what did you conclude from your literature survey?”

Three things. First, the field is overwhelmingly reactive — most systems recover after detection, and pay the recovery latency exactly when the deadline matters. Second, the one paper that does act early, FTAPA, shows that acting early genuinely reduces downtime — so the principle is sound. Third, and this is the gap: every attempt at prediction reduces an inherently uncertain judgement to one number compared against a tuned threshold, and both papers that do it report the tuning as an unsolved problem. Nobody treats node health as a latent variable with quantified uncertainty. That is what pointed us at Bayesian networks.

03c — Approach

Why a Bayesian Belief Network, and not something else

Expect “why did you choose this approach?” — and expect them to name an alternative. The answer is that four requirements fall out of the problem, and only one method meets all four.

The four requirements

  1. Works without a failure corpus — no labelled fog failure dataset exists.
  2. Survives partial telemetry — must still decide when a channel drops out.
  3. Emits a calibrated probability — the decision rule multiplies it by a cost.
  4. Is interpretable — an operator must be able to act on the reason, not just the number.
CandidateMeets?Principal reason
Fixed threshold rulesNoSingle metric, no uncertainty, silent under missing telemetry, threshold hard to tune — fails 1, 2 and 3.
Metaheuristics (PSO, ABC, GA)NoOptimises an assignment but never estimates whether a node will fail. Also heavy and parameter-sensitive.
Markov chains / fault treesPartlyState-space explosion at this size; poor with partial evidence; static structure. Weber et al. [8] document BNs displacing exactly these.
ML classifiers / deep learningPartlyNeeds thousands of labelled failures; must impute the very channels whose absence is diagnostic; confidence scores are miscalibrated; opaque.
Bayesian Belief NetworkYesPriors usable from day one; marginalises missing evidence; genuine calibrated posterior; causal chain explains each prediction.
Say this — “why not machine learning?”

Three reasons, and the second is the one that decides it. There is no labelled corpus of fog node failures to train on, whereas a Bayesian network can be seeded from published reliability priors and expert judgement and work from the first epoch. A classifier needs a value in every input slot — so when a telemetry channel goes silent it has to impute one, and a channel goes silent precisely because the node is in trouble. A Bayesian network marginalises over it instead. And our migration rule multiplies the output by a cost, so we need a real calibrated probability, which neural network confidence scores are famously not.

What to know — if they push on Markov chains or fault trees

These are the classical reliability instruments, so a mechanically-minded panel member may raise them. The answer is on your side: Weber et al. [8], which is in your reference list, surveys a decade of work and concludes that Bayesian networks have displaced Markov chains, fault trees and Petri nets for dependability and maintenance — because they model complex heterogeneous systems, reason both predictively and diagnostically, and update incrementally as evidence arrives. Citing your own reference to answer the objection is a strong move.

05 — Proposed work

The three novelties

If they ask “what is new here?”, give all three — a single novelty sounds thin for a final-year project, and the three are genuinely connected: one produces a probability, one spends it, one spends it more carefully.

N1

BBN-based failure prediction engine

A causal Bayesian Belief Network over fog-node telemetry infers P(failure within Δt | evidence). Missing evidence is marginalised out rather than imputed. The model is diagnostic as well as predictive, so it reports which condition is driving the risk. Conditional probability tables start from literature priors and are refined online from observed outcomes.

N2

Risk-aware placement and expected-loss migration

The posterior enters a placement utility alongside latency and energy, weighted per application class. Migration is triggered when the expected loss from staying exceeds the cost of moving — a decision-theoretic rule, not a threshold. This is the strongest answer in the whole project; see section 07.

N3

Criticality-aware selective replication

Standby capacity is allocated in proportion to risk × criticality, in three tiers: warm standby for high-risk critical modules, checkpoint-only for intermediate risk, nothing for best-effort. Directly attacks the “backup reserved whether or not it is needed” cost that [1], [3] and [5] all carry.

Say this — “which one is the main contribution?”

The Bayesian network is the core, because the other two only work once you have a calibrated probability rather than a binary alarm. But the framework is the contribution — a probability nobody acts on is not useful, and the expected-loss rule is what turns it into a decision.

06 — The technical core

How a Bayesian Belief Network actually works

This is the highest-risk area of the review. If one panel member knows probability, this is where they will probe. Be able to explain it without notes.

Say this — “explain a Bayesian Belief Network”

It is a directed acyclic graph where each node is a random variable and each edge means one variable directly influences another. Each node carries a conditional probability table giving its distribution for every combination of its parents’ values. Because the graph encodes conditional independence, the full joint distribution factorises into the product of those local tables — which is what makes it tractable. Given evidence on some variables, you can compute the posterior distribution of any other variable by summing out the ones you did not observe.

Our network has three tiers

  • Evidence (observed). CPU utilisation, RAM utilisation, tuple queue depth, uplink latency, jitter and packet loss, residual energy, temperature, handover rate.
  • Latent conditions. Computational stress, network degradation, energy stress, thermal stress. These are not measured — they are inferred.
  • Target. Node failure within the horizon Δt, also conditioned on a node-reliability prior from the device class and its historical MTBF.

Every variable is discretised to a small ordinal state space — typically {low, medium, high} — so the tables stay compact and readable.

Why the factorisation matters

With 13 variables at 3 states each, a full joint distribution would need roughly 1.6 million numbers. Factorised into local conditional tables over a sparse graph, it is a few hundred. That reduction is the whole reason Bayesian networks are usable, and it is a good thing to be able to state.

P(X₁ … Xn)  =  ∏i  P(Xi | parents(Xi))

Inference under partial evidence — the key property

If jitter is not reported this epoch, we do not guess a value for it. We sum over its possible states, weighted by its distribution, and the answer comes out as a slightly less sharp posterior:

P(F | eobserved)  =  Σunobserved  P(F, unobserved | eobserved)

A classifier — SVM, neural network — cannot do this. It needs a value in every input slot, so you have to impute one, and imputing a value for a channel that went silent because the node is in trouble is exactly the wrong guess. This is the single most persuasive argument for the choice of model, so lead with it.

Where the numbers come from

  • Structure: from domain knowledge — which conditions plausibly cause failure — not learned from data, because we have no failure corpus to learn it from.
  • Initial CPTs: seeded from the reliability literature in our own reference list (Weber et al. on dependability, Fenton et al. on reliability prediction, Pang & Abramov on IoT reliability) and expert elicitation over the device classes in the topology.
  • Refinement: Bayesian parameter learning with Dirichlet priors. Dirichlet is conjugate to the categorical distribution, so updating after observing outcomes is just adding counts — cheap enough to do online every epoch.
What to know — if pressed on CPT size

The failure node has five parents at three states, so a full table is 3⁵ = 243 rows. That is the largest table in the network; the latent nodes have two or three parents each, so 9–27 rows. If it grows, the standard remedy is a noisy-OR (or noisy-MAX) formulation, which reduces the parameter count from exponential to linear in the number of parents. Naming noisy-OR unprompted signals you understand the limitation.

07 — The best answer you have

“How do you choose the migration threshold?”

This question is almost certain, because every paper you surveyed struggles with it. Your answer is that you do not choose one — it falls out of the costs. Deliver this well and it will carry the review.

Migrating a running module is not free: it costs bandwidth, a brief service interruption, and possibly a worse host. So compare the expected loss of the two actions.

E[loss | stay]  =  P(fail | e) · Cfail       E[loss | migrate]  ≈  Cmigrate

Migrate when staying is worse:

P(fail | e) · Cfail  >  Cmigrate

Rearranged, the effective threshold on the probability is:

P*  =  Cmigrate  /  Cfail
Say this

We do not tune a threshold. The threshold is derived — it is just the ratio of the cost of migrating to the cost of failing. A module whose failure is cheap tolerates a high failure probability before we move it; a module whose failure is expensive gets moved on much weaker evidence. So every module effectively gets its own threshold, and it comes from the application’s own cost structure rather than from us picking a number.

What to know — the follow-up

If they ask “how do you get Cfail and Cmigrate in the same units?” — note that only the ratio matters, so any consistent normalised unit works. In our evaluation Cfail comes from deadline violations weighted by application criticality plus expected recovery time; Cmigrate from state transfer volume over available bandwidth plus the interruption window plus the utility lost on the new node. We will run a sensitivity analysis over the ratio.

Be ready for this — it is the weakest point in the project

There is no public fog-node failure trace for this topology. Do not hide it; state it first, before they find it.

Say this — “where is your failure data?”

There is no public failure dataset for a fog topology of this kind — that is a real limitation and we state it in the paper. So failures are injected synthetically by a harness we are adding to iFogSim2, and the conditional probability tables are seeded from the reliability literature before being adapted online. What to know — the sharp follow-up

If they say “isn’t that circular — you inject failures with a model and then predict them with a model?”, this is the answer: the two models are deliberately different in form. The harness fails nodes using a hazard function over the simulator’s internal state. The BBN never sees that — it only sees telemetry, which is sampled, discretised, delayed and sometimes missing. We also plan to test model mismatch on purpose, by injecting with a process whose structure the network does not assume, and we report calibration rather than just accuracy. This is standard practice in reliability simulation when field traces do not exist.

09 — Evaluation

How you will prove it works

You have no results yet, and that is correct for Review I. What you must have is a credible plan.

Prediction quality

  • Precision, recall, F1 of failure prediction within Δt
  • Calibration — does a stated 0.7 actually fail 70% of the time
  • Lead time — the interval between the decision rule firing and the actual failure
  • Robustness with telemetry channels deliberately withheld

System quality

  • Mean and tail application loop delay
  • Deadline-violation ratio for critical loops
  • Energy across cloud, fog and device tiers
  • Network usage, execution cost, service downtime
  • Redundancy overhead — reserved standby capacity
What to know — why lead time is the metric that matters

A prediction that arrives 50 ms before a failure is worthless if migration takes 200 ms. Accuracy alone does not capture that. Saying this unprompted shows you have thought past the textbook metrics — it is a genuinely good thing to volunteer.

Baselines

  1. Cloud-only placement
  2. iFogSim2 edgeward placement, no fault tolerance
  3. Reactive checkpointing with primary–backup replication — paper [1]
  4. Threshold-based proactive migration — FTAPA, paper [5]

Baseline 4 is the one that matters: it isolates the contribution of probabilistic inference from the contribution of merely acting early. Say that explicitly — it shows the comparison is designed to be hard on yourselves.

10 — Question bank

Everything else they might ask

Triaged by how likely each is. Study the red ones until fluent; skim the green ones so you are not blindsided.

Near certain Likely If they’re sharp

QWhy a Bayesian network and not machine learning or deep learning? Near certain

Full answer in section 03c. Four reasons, in order of strength. Data: a neural network needs thousands of labelled failures, and no such corpus exists for fog — a BBN can be seeded from expert knowledge and literature priors and still function on day one. Missing evidence: a BBN marginalises; a classifier needs imputation. Interpretability: we get the causal chain driving the risk, which an operator can act on. Calibration: our decision rule multiplies the output by a cost, so we need a genuine probability — neural network confidence scores are famously miscalibrated. Add that our own reference [10] takes the deep-learning route for IoT reliability, so we are aware of the alternative and chose against it deliberately.

QWhat have you actually completed so far? Near certain

Be precise and do not overstate. Literature survey of five fog fault-tolerance systems plus the BBN reliability foundations. iFogSim2 environment set up and running, with baseline fog-versus-cloud simulations executed for the VR game and surveillance applications across five scaling configurations. Problem formulation and framework design complete, and the paper drafted. The BBN implementation itself is Phase II — that is the plan, not a slip.

QWhy iFogSim2 and not a real testbed? Likely

A real fog testbed with enough nodes to observe meaningful failure statistics is beyond an undergraduate project’s resources, and you cannot induce failures on production hardware. iFogSim2 gives us the three things the evaluation needs — mobility from real traces, microservice orchestration, dynamic clustering — plus reproducibility. It is the standard tool in this literature, built on CloudSim.

QWhat is a microservice here, and what does migration actually move? Likely

An application is a directed graph of modules connected by edges carrying tuples — in iFogSim2 terms, AppModule and AppEdge, with AppLoop defining the paths whose latency we measure. A microservice is one such module placed on one fog device. Migration moves the module instance and its accumulated state to another device and re-routes the tuple flows to it; for a stateless module it is re-instantiation, and for a stateful one the state transfer is what dominates the migration cost.

QWhat if the prediction is wrong? Likely

Both error types are real and they are not symmetric. A false positive costs one unnecessary migration — bandwidth and a brief interruption. A false negative costs an unhandled failure. The expected-loss rule already encodes that asymmetry: because we multiply by Cfail, we accept more false positives for modules where failure is expensive. That is a deliberate design property, not an accident.

QWhat is the overhead of running inference every epoch? If they’re sharp

The network is small — around 13 variables in a layered, nearly-polytree structure — so exact inference by variable elimination is on the order of milliseconds. It runs once per node per decision epoch at the proxy tier, not per tuple. We will measure and report controller overhead as one of the system metrics rather than assuming it away.

QYour controller is a single point of failure. If they’re sharp

Correct, and worth conceding immediately. Three mitigations. The controller holds no critical state between epochs except the CPTs, which are synchronised to a cloud repository, so a replacement can start with current knowledge. Fog nodes fall back to their last known placement if the controller is unreachable, so the system is fail-safe rather than fail-stop. And distributed inference across clustered fog nodes is explicit future work — iFogSim2’s clustering support is what makes it feasible.

QWhy discretise? Doesn’t that throw away information? If they’re sharp

Yes, it does. Three responses. Bin edges are chosen from data quantiles rather than arbitrarily. Three states are sufficient because the output feeds a coarse decision — we need a probability, not a precise CPU figure. And Fenton et al., who are in our reference list, developed dynamic discretisation exactly for this problem, refining bins in the region of interest — that is our stated upgrade path.

QA static BBN has no notion of time, but degradation is temporal. If they’re sharp

A genuinely good question and one to concede cleanly. The current model gives a posterior per epoch. We partially compensate by including trend-derived evidence — rate of change, queue growth — rather than only instantaneous values. The correct full answer is a Dynamic Bayesian Network, which models the evolution of state across time slices, and that is named as future work in the paper.

QWhat about correlated failures — a whole rack or uplink going down? If they’re sharp

A naive network assumes conditional independence given parents, which correlated failures violate. But the fix is natural in this formalism: add a shared-cause latent node — cluster power or uplink health — as a common parent of the affected nodes. Expressing shared causes is one of the things Bayesian networks are actually good at, so it is an extension rather than a redesign.

QHow do you set the criticality weight κ? Isn’t it arbitrary? If they’re sharp

It is not ours to invent — it comes from the application’s service class, set by whoever owns the application, the same way QoS tiers are assigned in any prioritised system. For our evaluation we derive it from deadline tightness and consequence of loss: health monitoring highest, surveillance next, VR game lowest. We will report a sensitivity analysis over κ rather than claiming one setting is correct.

QDid you learn the graph structure or assume it? If they’re sharp

Assumed, from domain knowledge, because structure learning needs a substantial observational dataset that does not exist here. We validate it two ways: by expert review of the causal claims, and by sensitivity analysis — checking whether the posterior is unreasonably sensitive to any single edge or prior. Structure learning becomes possible once enough operational data has been accumulated through the online adaptation loop.

11 — Traps

Things not to say

Avoid these

  • Do not quote results you do not have. Review I is design-only. If asked “how much does it improve things?”, say the evaluation is Phase II and describe the metrics instead. Inventing a number is the fastest way to lose the panel.
  • Do not say “we use AI” or “we use machine learning” vaguely. Say Bayesian inference or probabilistic graphical model. The vague phrasing invites exactly the questions you least want.
  • Do not say the network “learns from our dataset.” You have no failure dataset. Say priors from literature, refined online from observed outcomes.
  • Do not oversell against FTAPA. It is a real contribution and acting early is its idea. Position yours as adding uncertainty quantification on top, not as replacing it.
  • Do not guess when you do not know. Say “we haven’t finalised that — our current thinking is X.” Panels reward a bounded honest answer and punish confident invention.
Say this — if genuinely stuck

That is not something we have settled yet. Our current thinking is [X], and it is on the list for Phase II — could we come back to you on it?
